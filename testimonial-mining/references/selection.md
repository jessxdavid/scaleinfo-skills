# Marking candidates and scoring them

Turning an hour of interview into a set of single-idea assets is a day's work if you do it by
feel. It is an afternoon if you do it in two passes: mark everything that could be a clip, then
score what you marked. Do not merge the passes. Marking while scoring produces four clips, all of
which do the same job, because the first strong moment sets the standard for what you notice next.

---

## 1. The marking pass

**Read the transcript, not the video.** Text is faster to scan, easier to search, and it stops you
being persuaded by a moment that only worked because of how they said it.

Mark **5–10 moments**. For each one: timestamp in, timestamp out, the quote as spoken.

### What makes a moment

- **A specific number.** A figure, a date, a count — said out loud by the client, not supplied by
  the interviewer.
- **A named before-and-after.** Where they were, where they are, how long it took. All three in
  one breath is the single most valuable shape in an interview.
- **A moment of doubt that resolved.** They nearly did not do it, and they say why they did. This
  is the shape that converts a sceptic, because the sceptic is currently living in the first half
  of that sentence.
- **The mechanism in their own words.** What actually changed in how they work. Worth more than
  the founder's version of the same explanation, because the client has nothing to gain by
  explaining it well.
- **An objection answered unprompted.** They raise it themselves and knock it down. Rarest, best,
  and the only source of an objection answer that does not sound defensive.

### The five-second test

If you can turn the moment into a headline in under five seconds, it is a candidate. If you are
still constructing the headline at ten seconds, the moment is doing too many things at once — it
is two clips, or it is nothing.

### The candidate table

Output this before scoring anything, so the user can see what you found and tell you what you
missed. They were in the room; they remember a line you skimmed past.

```
| # | In    | Out   | Type              | The quote, as spoken           |
|---|-------|-------|-------------------|--------------------------------|
| 1 | 04:12 | 04:38 | before-and-after  | "..."                          |
| 2 | 11:50 | 12:15 | doubt resolved    | "..."                          |
```

**Quote them exactly.** Not a tidy paraphrase — the words in the transcript, stumbles and all. The
cut is going to contain those words, so the plan has to contain them too. If a stumble makes the
quote hard to read on the page, mark it `[trims to: ...]` underneath rather than silently cleaning
it up.

---

## 2. Scoring

Three tests. Yes or no, one line each.

| Test | What it means | Failing it means |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Kills one named objection** | An objection from the user's own list, in a prospect's words | It is proof, not persuasion. Proof block, maybe an ad — never a Q&A answer |
| **2. Stands alone** | Someone who has never heard of the offer sees only these seconds and it makes sense | Not a retargeting ad. Might still work on the page, where there is context around it |
| **3. Carries specific proof** | A number, a date, a named outcome | Cannot lead a retargeting ad. "It changed my life" is a sentiment; `[$X]` in `[TIMEFRAME]` is proof |

**3/3** ships first, and usually serves two outputs.
**2/3** is usable — say which test it failed, because that decides where it lands.
**1/3** goes to the proof block or nowhere.

Put the scoring table in the handoff file, **including what you discarded and why**. Otherwise the
same clip gets re-argued in three weeks by someone scrolling the same transcript.

## 3. Weak moment or weak cut — decide this before discarding anything

This is where the time goes, and it is the distinction people get wrong. They spend an afternoon
re-cutting footage that was never going to work, and bin the clip that needed its first two
seconds moved.

> **A weak moment is unfixable. Discard it and move on.**
> **A weak cut of a strong moment is fixable. Re-cut it rather than binning it.**

### It is a weak moment when

- There is no number, no objection and no specific in it.
- The client is **agreeing rather than telling** — "yeah, definitely, it's been great" to a
  question that already did the work.
- It could have been said by anyone, about anything, about any offer.
- It is praise for the founder rather than a description of what changed. Warm, and worth nothing
  in an ad.
- It needs a paragraph of setup to land, and the setup is not in the footage.

No edit rescues any of these. It is not a cutting problem, it is a footage problem, and the fix is
in the next interview — hand the user the two questions from `interview-questions.md` that would
have produced the missing half.

### It is a weak cut when

The substance is right there — the number is said, the objection is knocked down — but:

- It **starts mid-sentence**, so the first frame is meaningless.
- It **runs past its own point**, trailing into "yeah, so, anyway".
- It only makes sense **if you heard the interviewer's question**.
- It carries **two arguments**, so neither one lands.

Every one of those is an in-point, an out-point or a line of on-screen text.

### Re-cutting — the four moves

1. **Move the in-point to where the client starts a sentence.** Not to where the interesting part
   starts. A clip that opens mid-clause reads as a fragment no matter how good the clause is.
2. **End on the claim.** Out-point goes immediately after the last word of the point, before the
   trailing agreement noise. Almost every baggy clip is baggy at the end, not the start.
3. **If it needs the question, put the question on screen — do not put it in the audio.** A
   line of text answers it in a second, and the clip keeps the client's voice throughout. Cutting
   the interviewer's voice in gives you two speakers in a thirty-second ad and halves its impact.
4. **If it carries two arguments, cut it twice.** Two clips, two hooks, two placements. Not one
   clip that does both jobs badly.

## 4. The second pass on the winners

Every video in this course has to clear four tests. For a client clip, two of them do most of the
work — run all four anyway, and be honest when one fails.

1. **Is it worth their time?** Decided in the first three seconds, before anything is absorbed.
   A testimonial earns this with the number, the before-state, or a doubt named so precisely it
   can only be about the viewer.
2. **Is the solution clear — does it take them from A to Z?** Testimonials rarely carry the full
   mechanism, and they are not supposed to. What they must not do is leave a gap the viewer has
   to go elsewhere to fill. Point forward instead of trailing off.
3. **Is it better than what is already in their head?** A clip that describes an outcome the
   viewer already believes is achievable changes nothing.
4. **Can they see themselves in it?** The hardest test for a testimonial and the one that decides
   the batch. Pick clients whose *starting point* looks like the viewer's, not clients whose
   ending looks most impressive. A result so far beyond the viewer that it reads as a different
   species converts worse than a modest one they can picture reaching.

**Test 4 is also an ordering rule.** When two clips are close, lead with the one whose before-state
is nearest the audience you are running it at.

## 5. What never becomes a clip

Discard these at the marking pass, before they get scored and argued over:

- **A number the user cannot back up.** If they cannot produce the screenshot, the invoice or the
  dashboard, it does not go in an ad. This is the same rule as never inventing a figure — a
  claim you cannot evidence is not improved by a real person saying it.
- **A claim the client makes about someone else.** Their own results only.
- **Anything the client has not consented to being used in paid ads.** Page use and ad use are
  different permissions.
- **A moment where the interviewer supplied the number** and the client repeated it. That is the
  user's claim wearing a client's face.
- **Anything identifying a third party** — a named employer, a client of the client, a platform
  account — that the user has not confirmed is fine to show.
- **A moment that only works with the founder's voice over it.** That is a founder ad. Script it
  in `ad-scripting` instead of forcing it out of this footage.

---

## 6. Honest yields

> **If a transcript yields nothing, say so.** Cutting filler to hit a number is how a library
> fills with assets nobody watches.

An honest *"there are three clips in this, not eight"* is right far more often than it feels. When
that happens, do two things in the same breath: hand over the three, and hand over the two or
three questions from `interview-questions.md` that would have produced the missing ones. The next
interview is the fix. The edit is not.
