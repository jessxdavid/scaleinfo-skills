# Running the interview so it yields cuttable footage

A clip plan is only as good as the footage. When an hour of interview yields two usable clips, it
is almost never because the client was dull — it is because of how the interview was run. Nearly
every fix is a change to how the question was asked, and every one of them is free.

Read this **before** the user films. If they have already filmed and the transcript under-yielded,
read the marked sections below and hand them the two or three questions that would have fixed it.

---

## 1. Record it so a transcript exists

This whole skill runs on text. Set that up before anything else.

- **Record it as a call, with the transcript on.** Zoom, Riverside, Google Meet, Loom — all of
  them will produce a timestamped transcript. Turn it on at the start, not at minute forty.
- **Record the client's audio on its own track** if the tool offers it. Separate tracks mean the
  interviewer's "mm-hm" over the top of the best sentence in the interview is removable rather
  than fatal.
- **Timestamps are the deliverable.** A transcript without them turns the clip plan into a
  scavenger hunt through an hour of footage.

**The shoot spec is the same one every video in this course runs on**, and one of its rules is
load-bearing here:

- **Shoot horizontal, crop to vertical afterwards.** Horizontal crops to 9:16 in one click.
  Vertical footage can never become a long-form breakdown, a page video or anything on a desktop.
  Film vertical and the one-library-three-outputs premise is gone.
- **Camera at eye level or slightly above.** Never looking up at them.
- **Light from 45 degrees, never straight on.** Straight-on light is flat.
- **A clip-on mic beats everything you can do afterwards.** Fix the room, not the file — a great
  mic in an echoing room still sounds bad.
- **Highest resolution the device offers.** In the UK and Europe, film at 25fps or mains lighting
  flickers.

> **The bar is clear, not slick.** Unpolished proof reads as true; a client who looks
> professionally produced reads as an actor. Do not fix this by making it look worse on purpose —
> just do not spend a week on it.

---

## 2. The four rules that decide whether the footage is cuttable

These matter more than the question list. All four are about the shape of the answer, not its
content.

**1. Ask them to answer in full sentences, restating the question.**
Say it out loud before you start, and say it again the first time they forget:

> *"Answer like I'm not here — so instead of 'about four months', say 'it took me about four
> months'. It just means I can use the clip on its own."*

This single instruction is the difference between a cut that stands alone and a cut that only
makes sense if you heard the question. It is the most common reason a strong moment becomes a
weak cut, and it costs one sentence to prevent.

**2. Do not talk over the answer.** Leave three seconds of silence when they finish. Most people
fill it with the specific they left out the first time — the number, the date, the detail. The
interviewer's job in that silence is to say nothing.

**3. Ask for the number twice.** Once as the answer, once as the clip:

> *"And just so I've got it clean — say that as one sentence: where you started, where you are
> now, and how long it took."*

Nobody minds. It takes eight seconds and it produces the one line the retargeting ad is built on.

**4. One idea per answer.** If they are three points into an answer, let them finish, then ask the
second point again as its own question. A moment that carries two arguments is two clips, and
cutting it in the edit is far harder than asking again in the room.

## 3. The question set, grouped by the output it feeds

Ask them roughly in this order. It walks the client through their own story, which is also the
order the clips will eventually be watched in.

### Before — feeds the retargeting ad and the proof block

- Where were you before we started — what was actually going on?
- What had you already tried, and what happened with it?
- What was the moment you decided you had to do something different?

*The "what had you already tried" answer is worth waiting for.* It is where the objection
"I've tried things like this before" gets killed by someone who is not the founder.

### The doubt — feeds the objection answers

- What nearly stopped you from signing up?
- What were you sceptical about?
- What did you think was going to go wrong that didn't?

*These three questions produce more usable clips than any other block in the interview*, because
a client naming their own hesitation is the only source of an objection answer that does not sound
defensive. If the interview is short on time, cut something else.

### The mechanism, in their words — feeds beat 2 of the pre-call sequence

- What actually changed in how you work day to day?
- What's the one thing you do now that you weren't doing before?
- If you were explaining it to someone in your position, how would you describe it?

*A client explaining the system is worth more than the founder explaining it*, because they have
nothing to gain by explaining it well.

### The numbers — feeds everything

- Where were you at when we started, and where are you now?
- Over what period?
- What's the number you'd point at if someone asked whether it worked?

**Never feed them a figure.** Not "so that's about a 3x, right?" A prompted number that the client
then repeats is the user's number in the client's mouth, and it is exactly the thing that turns a
testimonial into a problem. Ask, wait, and write down whatever they say.

### The objection, answered unprompted — the rarest and best clips

Bring the real objection list into the room and ask them directly:

> *"People who are where you were a year ago tell us [objection, in their words]. What would you
> say to them?"*

Ask it once per objection, for the three or four that come up most. The answers are the highest-
value thirty seconds in the entire recording, and they only exist if somebody asks the question.

### The close

- What would you say to someone sitting on the fence right now?
- Is there anything I haven't asked that you'd want someone to know?

The second question regularly produces the best clip in the interview. Ask it every time.

## 4. Where the objection list comes from

Do not invent the objections to ask about. They are already written down, in four places:

- **The last ten sales call recordings.** Every question asked before the prospect decided. The
  ones that come up three times are the first three to ask a client about.
- **The setter's notes.** Main problem, previous attempts, what is holding them back — that is a
  list of objections in the prospect's own words.
- **DMs, ad comments and email replies.** The question someone types at 11pm is the one blocking
  the sale.
- **No-shows and lost calls.** Ask the ones who did not buy what they were unsure about.

Use the exact wording the prospect used. *"Will this actually work for a plumber?"* makes a better
clip than *"niche suitability"*.

---

## 5. Consent, and one honest limit

- **Get permission in writing to use the footage in paid ads**, not just on the page. "Happy for
  you to use this" in a text message is enough; assuming it is not. This matters more for the
  retargeting output than the others, because that one puts their face in front of strangers.
- **Do not coach the answer.** Prompting a client toward a phrasing produces a clip that sounds
  prompted, and viewers hear it.
- **If the client cannot produce a number, the interview is a testimonial, not a case study.** It
  still earns its place in the confirmation-page proof block, where volume is the mechanism. It
  will not carry a retargeting ad on its own. Say that plainly rather than stretching a warm
  quote into an ad it cannot support.

## Getting the transcript

The skill reads a transcript, not video. Getting one is a two-minute step and most people have
already done it without noticing.

- **Look where it was recorded first.** Loom, Zoom cloud recordings, Google Meet, Riverside and
  Descript all produce transcripts. Availability varies by plan and by setting, so check rather
  than assume.
- **The fallback that always works:** upload to YouTube as **unlisted**, wait for automatic
  captions, then **Show transcript** and copy it. Free, no new account for anyone with a Google
  login, and unlisted keeps it off the channel and out of search.
- **Keep timestamps** if you have them. They turn the clip plan into a cut list an editor can
  work straight from. Without them, quote the opening words of each clip instead.
- **Check the numbers against the recording.** Automatic captions get figures, names and jargon
  wrong, and figures are the whole point here. Confirm every number before it reaches an ad.

If the interview was recorded on a phone with no transcript at all, the YouTube route still
works — upload the file, unlisted, same steps.
